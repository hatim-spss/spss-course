Part 2: Testing your data

Tests fall into two families. Parametric tests assume numeric data that is roughly normal (check with Lesson 5). Non-parametric tests make fewer assumptions and suit non-normal, ranked or categorical data.

PART A: PARAMETRIC TESTS

Assumptions for all of these: numeric outcome, roughly normal data, independent observations (except the repeated tests). If normality fails, use the non-parametric partner in Part B.

1. One-sample t-test

Use when: you compare one group's average to a known value (for example, is the class average different from 50?).

  1. Analyze → Compare Means → One-Sample T Test.
  2. Move your variable to Test Variable(s), type the known value in Test Value, click OK.

Read: Sig. (2-tailed) below .05 means the average differs from the test value.

Report: The mean score (M = __, SD = __) differed significantly from 50, t(df) = __, p = __.

2 and 3. Independent and paired t-tests

See Lesson 6 and Lesson 7.

4. One-way ANOVA

Use when: you compare the average of 3 or more separate groups.

  1. Analyze → Compare Means → One-Way ANOVA.
  2. Outcome into Dependent List, grouping variable into Factor.
  3. Click Options: tick Descriptive, Homogeneity of variance test and Welch. Continue.
  4. Click Post Hoc: tick Tukey. Continue, then OK.

Read:

  • Levene's test Sig. above .05: use the normal ANOVA table. Below .05: use the Welch result.
  • ANOVA Sig. below .05: at least one group differs.
  • Then use the Post Hoc (Tukey) table to see which groups differ.

Report: There was a significant difference between groups, F(df between, df within) = __, p = __.

Tip: Only run post hoc tests if the ANOVA itself is significant.

5. Repeated measures ANOVA

Use when: the same people are measured 3 or more times (for example, week 1, week 4, week 8).

  1. Data layout: one row per person, one column per time point.
  2. Analyze → General Linear Model → Repeated Measures.
  3. Type a factor name (for example Time), enter the number of levels, click Add, then Define.
  4. Move your time columns into Within-Subjects Variables.
  5. Click Options: tick Descriptive statistics and Estimates of effect size. Continue, then OK.

Read:

  • Mauchly's test Sig. above .05: use the Sphericity Assumed row.
  • Mauchly's Sig. below .05: use the Greenhouse-Geisser row.
  • Sig. below .05 in Tests of Within-Subjects Effects means scores changed over time.

Report: Scores changed significantly over time, F(df, df error) = __, p = __, partial η² = __.

6. Two-way ANOVA

Use when: you have one numeric outcome and two grouping factors (for example, Gender and Teaching method).

  1. Analyze → General Linear Model → Univariate.
  2. Outcome into Dependent Variable, both factors into Fixed Factor(s).
  3. Plots: put one factor on the horizontal axis and the other in Separate Lines, click Add, Continue.
  4. Options: tick Descriptive statistics, Estimates of effect size, Homogeneity tests. Continue, then OK.

Read: Check the interaction row first (Factor1 * Factor2). If it is significant, the effect of one factor depends on the other, so interpret the plot. If not, read each main effect.

Tip: Adding a numeric Covariate in the same window turns this into an ANCOVA.

7. Pearson correlation

Use when: you test the linear relationship between two numeric variables.

  1. Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate.
  2. Move both variables across, tick Pearson and Two-tailed, click OK.
r (ignore the sign)Strength
.10Small
.30Medium
.50 or moreLarge

Read: The sign shows direction (positive: both rise together; negative: one rises as the other falls). Sig. below .05 means the relationship is significant.

Report: Age and score were positively correlated, r(df) = __, p = __.

Tip: Correlation does not prove one thing causes the other.

8. Linear regression (simple and multiple)

Use when: you want to predict a numeric outcome from one predictor (simple) or several (multiple).

  1. Analyze → Regression → Linear.
  2. Outcome into Dependent, predictor(s) into Independent(s).
  3. For multiple regression, click Statistics and tick Collinearity diagnostics. Continue, then OK.

Read three tables:

  • Model Summary: R Square is the share of the outcome explained by your predictors.
  • ANOVA: Sig. below .05 means the model predicts better than chance.
  • Coefficients: B shows how much the outcome changes per one-unit rise in the predictor. Sig. shows which predictors matter.

Report: The model was significant, F(df, df) = __, p = __, R² = __. Age significantly predicted score (B = __, p = __).

Tip: In multiple regression, a VIF above 10 (or Tolerance below .1) means your predictors overlap too much.

PART B: NON-PARAMETRIC TESTS

Use these when: data is not normal, the sample is very small, the data is ranked (1st, 2nd, 3rd) or the variables are categories.

9. Mann-Whitney U (partner of the independent t-test)

  1. Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs → 2 Independent Samples.
  2. Outcome into Test Variable List, grouping variable into Grouping Variable, click Define Groups.
  3. Tick Mann-Whitney U, then OK.

Read: Asymp. Sig. (2-tailed) below .05 means the groups differ. Check the Ranks table for the group with the higher mean rank.

Report: Scores differed significantly between groups, U = __, Z = __, p = __.

10. Wilcoxon signed-rank (partner of the paired t-test)

  1. Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs → 2 Related Samples.
  2. Move the before and after variables into Test Pairs, tick Wilcoxon, click OK.

Read: Asymp. Sig. (2-tailed) below .05 means the scores changed.

Report: Scores changed significantly after training, Z = __, p = __.

11. Kruskal-Wallis (partner of one-way ANOVA)

  1. Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs → K Independent Samples.
  2. Outcome into Test Variable List, grouping variable into Grouping Variable.
  3. Click Define Range (minimum and maximum group codes), tick Kruskal-Wallis H, click OK.

Read: Asymp. Sig. below .05 means at least one group differs. To find which, run Mann-Whitney U on each pair and use a stricter limit: divide .05 by the number of pairs (Bonferroni).

Report: Scores differed across groups, H(df) = __, p = __.

12. Friedman test (partner of repeated measures ANOVA)

  1. Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs → K Related Samples.
  2. Move all time-point columns into Test Variables, tick Friedman, click OK.

Read: Asymp. Sig. below .05 means scores changed across time points.

Report: Scores differed over time, χ²(df) = __, p = __.

13. Spearman correlation (partner of Pearson)

  1. Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate.
  2. Move both variables across, untick Pearson, tick Spearman, click OK.

Read: Same as Pearson. The coefficient is called rho (ρ).

Report: Age and score were positively correlated, ρ = __, p = __.

14. Chi-square tests (categorical data)

A. Test of independence: are two categorical variables related (for example, Gender and Pass/Fail)?

  1. Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs.
  2. One variable into Row(s), the other into Column(s).
  3. Statistics: tick Chi-square and Phi and Cramer's V. Continue.
  4. Cells: tick Expected and Row percentages. Continue, then OK.

Read:

  • Check the footnote: no more than 20% of cells should have an expected count below 5.
  • Use the Pearson Chi-Square row. For a 2x2 table, use Continuity Correction. If the footnote assumption fails, use Fisher's Exact Test.
  • Sig. below .05 means the variables are related. Cramér's V shows strength (about .1 small, .3 medium, .5 large).

Report: Gender and pass status were significantly associated, χ²(df, N = __) = __, p = __, V = __.

B. Goodness of fit: do the categories occur equally often, or as expected?

  1. Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs → Chi-square.
  2. Move your categorical variable across. Choose All categories equal or type your expected values, then OK.

Read: Asymp. Sig. below .05 means the counts differ from what was expected.

15. McNemar test (paired categories)

Use when: the same people answer a yes/no question twice (for example, before and after a lecture).

  1. Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs.
  2. Put the "before" variable in Rows and "after" in Columns.
  3. Statistics: tick McNemar. Continue, then OK.

Read: Sig. below .05 means the proportion changed between the two times.

Parametric vs non-parametric: quick summary

ParametricNon-parametric
DataNumeric, roughly normalAny shape, ranks or categories
ComparesMeansRanks or counts
PowerStronger when assumptions are metSafer when they are not
Sample sizePrefers larger samplesWorks with small samples
